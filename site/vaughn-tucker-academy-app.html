Chapter One
One-Point Perspective

Look straight into this simple room. The back wall faces you. Its horizontal and vertical edges stay horizontal and vertical; the room’s depth edges meet at one vanishing point.

Teaching diagramThis is a constructed room, not an overlay on an original artwork. Move the red point to explore the view.

1. Find the eye-level line and the red vanishing point.

2. Follow the floor and ceiling edges toward that point.

3. Notice that the back wall remains a rectangle.

4. Draw a room of your own. Check only the edges receding directly away from you against this vanishing point.

Chapter Two
Two-Point Perspective

Look at the corner of this building block. One family of horizontal edges recedes left; the other recedes right. Upright edges remain vertical.

Teaching diagramThe two points always share one eye-level line. This is an idealized construction. Apply it to real artwork in the Kingstown lesson.

1. Find the nearest upright corner.

2. Follow both edges of the left wall toward the blue point.

3. Follow both edges of the right wall toward the red point.

4. Move the eye level. Check when you can see the top or underside.

Chapter Three — Advanced
Three-Point Perspective
Look up at a skyscraper — its sides taper upward to a third vanishing point. Used for dramatic worm's-eye or bird's-eye views.
The dramatic viewSkyscrapers, aerial city views, extreme close-ups. Three VPs — upright edges converge toward the third point.
1
VP-Left and VP-Right on the horizon — same as 2PP.
2
Add VP-Up far above the horizon — a third point.
3
All "vertical" edges now angle toward VP-Up. The center edge may still appear vertical when it aligns with the third point.
4
Corner edge aims toward VP-Up; it may look vertical when directly aligned with that point.
5
Drag the green VP-Up dot on the canvas — feel the tilt!

Chapter Four — Project
City Street in Two-Point Perspective
A city block is the ultimate 2PP exercise. All buildings share the same two VPs — only heights change. Click each step to build the scene.
Golden ruleEvery building on the same block shares the exact same two VPs. Lock them in first, then vary heights freely.
1
Horizon + two VPs far off each side. Low horizon = dramatic street view.
2
Street & curbs — road and sidewalk edges converge to both VPs.
3
Building corner verticals — vary heights. Connect each top/bottom to both VPs.
4
Far edges & rooflines — cut across guide lines with a vertical to end each building.
5
Windows — horizontal lines aim at the same VP as the wall they sit on.
6
Cornices & awnings — decorative ledges follow the VP lines too.
7
Shade & render — hatch shadow faces, add lamppost, figures.
Pencil tipConstruction lines = feather-light. Final outlines = firm pressure. Erase guides last.
Acrylic School — Lesson One
One-Point Perspective in Paint
Vaughn's acrylic painting "To St. Elizabeth" — a Jamaican road pulling you through a stone tunnel arch. Everything in this painting rushes toward one single point. This is 1PP at its most dramatic.
What makes this acrylicNotice the bold, confident strokes. Acrylic dries fast — Vaughn had to commit to each decision quickly. The lush greens were built up in layers, lights over darks.
1
Find the vanishing point — it sits dead centre in the tunnel opening. Every element aims at it.
2
The road is your guide — both edges and the dashed centreline converge perfectly to the VP.
3
The tunnel arch frames it — a circle in 1PP stays a circle. Only the rectangular bridge walls above recede.
4
The truck is the payoff — tiny, centred, bathed in light beyond the darkness. Vaughn placed it exactly on the VP to draw your eye through.
5
Toggle Guides off — study how Vaughn handled the tropical foliage. No perspective rules there — pure observation and bold acrylic strokes.
